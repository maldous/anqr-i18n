4	"ANQR (pronounced \"anchor\") is a free QR code generator that creates scannable codes for URLs, text, Wi-Fi credentials, contact cards, calendar events, and more."

13	"How ANQR works"
14	"ANQR runs in your browser and encodes your input into a standard QR matrix with Reed–Solomon error correction, then renders it to the format you choose."
15	"When you add overlays or animations, ANQR balances styling with scan reliability so the result remains scannable."
16	"ANQR focuses on static codes (the data is inside the QR). If you need a dynamic code (editable destination), you can encode your own redirect URL or short link."

92	"QR Encoding Settings"
93	"Version: QR codes come in versions 1-40, with higher versions holding more data but being larger. Set to 0 (Auto) to let ANQR choose the smallest version that fits your content."
94	"Error Correction: Determines how much damage a QR code can sustain while remaining scannable."
95	"L (Low): 7% error correction - smallest size, least redundancy."
96	"M (Medium): 15% error correction - balanced option."
97	"Q (Quartile): 25% error correction - good for printed codes."
98	"H (High): 30% error correction - best for codes with overlays or in harsh conditions."
99	"Quiet Zone (Margin)"
100	"The quiet zone is the white space around the QR code. Scanners need this margin to detect where the code starts. The standard recommends at least 4 modules. Reducing below 4 may cause scanning issues."

686	"Upgrading an existing QR campaign is one of the highest-leverage things a small business can do: you keep the customer journey you already rely on, but you improve trust, scan reliability, and measurement. 687	"Why upgrade existing QR codes?"
688	"Most businesses already have QR codes \"in the wild\" - on menus, windows, packaging, posters, and counter stands. Over time those codes can become fragile (bad prints, glare, low contrast) and off-brand (generic generator defaults). A thoughtful uplift can increase scan confidence, reduce friction at the point of sale, and make the QR feel like a deliberate part of your marketing rather than a leftover utility."
689	"Auditing your current QR codes"
690	"Start by making an inventory. Scan every QR you can find and record: where it’s placed, what it links to, whether it’s permanent or temporary, and how reliable it feels under real conditions. Take photos of each placement (lighting and distance matter), and note any that require multiple attempts to scan. This audit tells you which codes are safe to replace quickly and which need a careful rollout plan."
691	"Safe replacement strategy"
692	"For permanent placements (signage, packaging, long-lived posters), aim for \"same destination, better code\": keep the encoded URL the same and regenerate with stronger safety settings. If you need to change destinations in the future, introduce a stable redirect URL and treat that as the long-term anchor. Always print a single proof first, test it in the real environment, then commit to the full run."
693	"Adding branding safely"
694	"Branding is valuable when it increases trust, not when it makes the code delicate. Use conservative colour contrast, keep overlays small and centred, and never modify finder patterns. If you’re styling for a busy surface (wood grain, patterned menu, textured wall), add a clean backing shape behind the code so the camera sees clear boundaries."
695	"Print vs digital considerations"
696	"Print and screen behave differently. Print introduces glare, smudges, and resolution problems; screens introduce refresh artefacts, compression, and brightness extremes. Choose output formats accordingly (SVG for print, tested raster/video for screens) and size for the real scanning distance. If a QR is meant to be scanned from a queue, it needs to be large enough that a customer can capture it without stepping out of line."

698	"Treat testing as part of the campaign, not an afterthought. Test across multiple phones and scanner apps, in the real lighting, from the real distance, and with the real signage material. If the QR is business-critical (payments, check-in, onboarding), test failure modes too: low battery camera, older devices, glare, and hurried scanning. Once validated, lock the configuration via a share link so future reprints don’t drift."

717	"Why SVG for print?"
718	"SVG is ideal because it’s resolution-independent. A QR exported as SVG can be scaled to any physical size without losing the crisp module edges scanners rely on. 720	"PNG is acceptable when you control the final size and export at that exact size (or larger) with sufficient pixels. It’s commonly required by some platforms and template systems, but it becomes dangerous when people resize it later. If you must distribute a PNG, include the intended physical size and a note: \"Do not scale up\"."

726	"DPI matters only for raster exports. For PNG, choose enough pixels that each module remains crisp at the final physical size - and avoid any scaling after export. 963	"Why Vector (SVG) Is Preferred for Print"
964	"SVG files describe QR codes as mathematical shapes rather than grids of pixels. This means the code can be scaled to any size - from a 1cm label to a 10-metre banner - without any loss of edge sharpness. The printer's RIP (Raster Image Processor) renders the vectors at the output device's native resolution, ensuring each module edge is as crisp as the hardware allows. There's no interpolation, no anti-aliasing artifacts, no accumulated blur from multiple resize operations."
965	"Beyond scalability, SVG files are more robust in professional workflows. They survive round-trips through design software without degradation. They can be colour-corrected without resampling. They embed cleanly into PDF files for press-ready output. For any print application where quality matters, SVG should be your default export format."
966	"When PNG Is Acceptable"
967	"PNG becomes necessary when your workflow or destination platform doesn't support vector formats. Some web platforms, signage CMS systems, and legacy print workflows require raster images. In these cases, PNG can work well - but only if you follow strict rules: export at the final physical size and resolution, never scale up after export, and avoid any lossy compression or format conversion."
968	"The critical rule for PNG is to calculate your required pixel dimensions before exporting. If your QR will print at 5cm and your printer operates at 300 DPI, you need approximately 590 pixels. Export at this size or larger, then scale down if needed - never up. Include this information in your filename or metadata so future users don't accidentally misuse a size-specific export."

1020	"Understanding Error Correction Levels"
1021	"QR codes include built-in redundancy that allows them to be read even when partially damaged or obscured. The error correction level - L (7%), M (15%), Q (25%), or H (30%) - determines how much of the code can be missing while still decoding correctly. Higher error correction creates larger codes but provides a safety margin for overlays, printing imperfections, and environmental damage."
1022	"For codes with image overlays, error correction level H is essential - the overlay physically obscures part of the code, and you need that 30% redundancy to maintain reliability. For clean, unstyled codes in controlled environments, level M often suffices. Level L should be reserved for situations where code size is critically constrained and you can guarantee pristine conditions."
1023	"The Critical Quiet Zone"
1024	"The quiet zone is the blank margin surrounding every QR code. Scanners use this boundary to identify where the code begins and ends. The ISO standard specifies a minimum quiet zone of four modules (four times the width of the smallest square in the code). Violating this space - with design elements, trim edges, or adjacent content - is one of the most common causes of scan failures."
1025	"ANQR's quiet zone enforcement helps maintain this critical spacing, but you must also ensure it's preserved in your final designs. When handing off QR artwork, explicitly specify the quiet zone requirements. When placing codes in layouts, verify that no elements intrude into this space. A few millimetres of clear space can mean the difference between reliable scanning and frustrated customers."
